ARTIFACT 01 · AI LAB

Security Alert Helper: An AI Chatbot for Junior SOC Analysts

A working AI chatbot I built that helps new Security Operations Center (SOC) analysts understand security alerts and decide what to do first.

Try the Live Chatbot ↗ Download Documentation (PDF)

Description

Users paste a security alert or describe suspicious activity. The chatbot explains what the alert means in simple language, rates how serious it is (Low, Medium, or High) with a reason, lists 3 to 5 first investigation steps, and reminds the user to follow their organization's incident response policy. For example, when I tested it with multiple failed admin logins from an unknown IP, it identified a likely brute force attack, rated it High, and suggested checking for successful logins, checking the IP's reputation, and looking for password spraying. The full project documentation, including all tests and results, is available to download above.

Objective

I created this chatbot to solve a real problem I saw during my SOC training: new analysts receive many technical alerts and are often unsure how serious they are or what to do first. I wanted to see whether a simple AI assistant could help them respond faster and with more confidence.

Tools and Technologies Used

Poe Prompt BotGemini 3 FlashDesign ThinkingMicrosoft Word

Process

I used design thinking. I started with the struggles of new analysts, wrote a clear problem statement, and compared three assistant ideas before choosing this one. I built the bot on Poe and wrote specific instructions for how it should respond, then added a greeting message. I ran into real obstacles: my first attempt did not create a shareable bot, the default model cost more points than my free account allowed, and my daily points ran out during testing. I rebuilt the bot with a lower-cost model and planned testing around the daily reset. Finally, I tested it with realistic security alerts and an off-topic question to check that it followed its instructions.

Value Proposition

This chatbot gives junior SOC analysts a fast, plain-language guide to any security alert, helping them prioritize threats and take the right first steps without waiting for a senior analyst.

Unique Value: Combines my cybersecurity training with hands-on generative AI skills. It is a live product that anyone can try, not just a description of an idea, and it shows how I adapted when free-tool limits got in the way.

Relevance: Security teams receive more alerts than they can easily handle, and AI assistants are becoming common tools in SOC work. This project shows skills that matter for both cybersecurity and AI development roles, while keeping human judgment and official procedures at the center.

ARTIFACT 02 · AI/ML ANALYSIS

Choosing the Right AI: Machine Learning vs. Deep Learning

A case analysis that compares two real-world AI systems and explains why one problem calls for traditional machine learning and the other needs deep learning.

View the Case Study → Download Full Report (PDF)

Description

This project compares two AI systems from my coursework and explains which approach fits each problem. The first is customer churn prediction, where a support vector machine (SVM) predicts which subscribers are likely to cancel so a company can offer them deals first. The second is medical image recognition, where a convolutional neural network (CNN) detects diabetic eye disease from retinal photos at the level of eye specialists. For each case, I explain why the chosen approach works and why the other approach would not. The full report is available to download above.

Objective

Choosing the wrong model wastes time, data, and computing power. I wanted to show that I can look at a real problem and decide which AI approach fits it before anything is built, and back up that decision with credible evidence.

Tools and Technologies Used

Support Vector MachinesConvolutional Neural NetworksPeer-Reviewed ResearchAPA StyleMicrosoft Word

Process

I started with the machine learning and deep learning examples from my course and selected one of each. I then searched for real-world applications backed by strong evidence rather than marketing claims, using a peer-reviewed churn study, a peer-reviewed study in JAMA, and an FDA announcement. I compared the two cases using four questions: what the data looks like, how much data exists, whether decisions must be explained, and how much computing power is needed. The hardest part was explaining why the other approach would not work, which required thinking carefully about data type, data size, explainability, and cost.

Value Proposition

This analysis gives teams a simple, evidence-based way to decide between traditional machine learning and deep learning before they invest time and money in building a model.

Unique Value: My first artifact shows that I can build an AI tool. This one shows a different skill: analyzing a problem and choosing the right AI method, explained in plain language that non-technical decision-makers can follow.

Relevance: Organizations in security, healthcare, and business all need people who can match AI methods to real problems. Knowing when a simpler model is the better choice saves resources and keeps decisions explainable.

References

AIML-500 Machine Learning vs. Deep Learning lesson and course materials; Coussement & Van den Poel (2008), Expert Systems with Applications; Gulshan et al. (2016), JAMA; U.S. Food and Drug Administration (2018)
