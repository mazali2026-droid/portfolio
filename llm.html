Overview

For this class activity I worked through a structured, four-stage dialogue with an AI tutor ("Professor Predict") running on Claude. Instead of reading about model training, I played the role of a language model, then used what I experienced to reason about training data and about which real tasks an LLM can handle reliably.

My first two portfolio artifacts focused on building an AI tool and comparing machine learning with deep learning. This one shows a different skill: evaluating the reliability of AI output, which is essential for anyone who deploys or depends on these systems.

What Happened

Stage 1: The Velmora trap

I completed "The capital of France is ___" with Paris and "Once upon a ___" with time. Both are strong, familiar patterns. Then I was asked for "the current president of the small island nation of Velmora." After first saying I didn't know, I guessed "Dead."

The twist: Velmora does not exist. My answer felt like knowledge, but nothing was behind it. That is a hallucination seen from the inside, and it is exactly what a language model does when a question has an answer-shaped gap.

Stage 2: Why it happens

When asked why a model would confidently answer a made-up question, I explained that it is built that way. Pretraining rewards predicting a likely next token, not checking whether it is true. Post-training helps models follow instructions and sometimes admit uncertainty, but there is no guaranteed fact-checker inside the model.

Why This Matters for My Career

I am working toward a role in cybersecurity or AI development. In a security operations center, analysts increasingly use AI assistants to explain alerts, summarize logs, and draft incident notes. This activity changed how I think about those tools:

  • Live threat data needs live sources. A model cannot know about an indicator of compromise or vulnerability published after its training. Threat intelligence feeds, SIEM data, and search must supply that.
  • Exact values need tools. IP addresses, hashes, timestamps, and counts should come from the logs or be computed, not recalled by the model.
  • Confident is not the same as correct. An AI that invents a plausible explanation for an alert is a security risk. Its output should be treated as a draft to verify.

This also connects to my first artifact, the Security Alert Helper chatbot. A tool like that is most useful when it explains well-documented concepts and works from the alert data it is given, and least reliable when asked to recall facts it was never provided.
